Let’s start with the injury, because this one matters. Luther Burden III suffered a concussion late in Chicago’s 27 to 24 win over Tampa Bay, and head coach Ben Johnson confirmed the diagnosis after the game.

That’s a tough development for a young receiver who was having an excellent night. Burden had scored a touchdown earlier in the game, giving Bears fans yet another look at the playmaking ability that makes him such an exciting part of this offense. Every time he touches the football, something can happen. He’s slippery, he’s explosive, and he plays with an edge. He was having one of those games where you could feel the confidence building.

But late in the fourth quarter, everything changed. Burden caught a screen pass and did what he always does: he fought for extra yards. He refused to go down easy. And that’s when Buccaneers defender Rueben Bain Jr. delivered a hard hit that involved contact with Burden’s helmet. The football came loose, and Burden stayed down on the ground immediately afterward.

And Bears fans, I know turnovers matter, especially late in a close game. We’ve already talked about the five turnovers in this one, and that fumble was part of it. But when a player takes a hit like that, the fumble stops being the biggest concern. Honestly, in that moment, nobody in Chicago was thinking about the ball. You just want to know whether he’s okay. You want to see him get up. You want to see him walk off the field on his own.

Here’s the important update. This is no longer just a suspected concussion. Ben Johnson confirmed it. That means Burden must satisfy the NFL’s concussion protocol before he can return to the field, and there’s no guaranteed timeline. None. That’s how this works, and that’s how it should work.

Now, there is a silver lining here, and it’s the timing. Chicago’s bye week comes at a really helpful moment. Burden won’t miss a game in Week 10, because there is no game in Week 10. That gives him extra time to recover before the Bears return to action. But I want to be careful here, because the bye week doesn’t automatically mean he’ll be cleared for the next game. A lot of fans are going to assume that two weeks off means he’s good to go. That’s not guaranteed.

Concussions are different for every single player. Recovery depends on symptoms, medical evaluations, and the league’s step-by-step return-to-participation process. Research on NFL players shows that many return within about two weeks, with one study putting the average recovery period a little longer than that. That’s useful background information. But it is not a prediction for Luther Burden, and I don’t want the Bears treating it like one. Every brain is different. Every injury is different.

This is a young player with a whole lot of football ahead of him. A whole career. Chicago needs to protect his health first and worry about the receiving rotation second. Period. If that means he sits an extra week, then he sits an extra week. No game in November is worth risking a player’s long-term health.

Now, from a football standpoint, losing Burden for any amount of time would still be significant. Let’s not pretend otherwise. He’s the kind of receiver who can turn a short pass into a big gain. That screen pass that led to the injury? That’s exactly the kind of play the Bears love to run for him, because he’s dangerous in space. He creates problems for defenders after the catch. He breaks tackles, makes people miss, and turns nothing into something. And his presence gives Caleb Williams another dangerous option whenever defenses focus their attention on the rest of Chicago’s playmakers.

That’s why this situation matters beyond one game. If Burden isn’t available when the Bears return from the bye, the offense is going to need to adjust. And that brings us to two receivers who could see more opportunities.

First up, Kalif Raymond. According to the report, Raymond ran routes on 77 percent of Chicago’s passing plays in Week 9. Seventy-seven percent. He also received seven targets and caught four passes. Those numbers tell you something important: he’s already trusted to handle a meaningful role in this offense. This isn’t a guy sitting at the end of the bench. He’s already in the mix.

If Burden misses time, Raymond could take on additional work from the slot. And that’s not an easy assignment. Slot receivers have to read coverage quickly. They have to find space in traffic, often with linebackers and safeties lurking all around them. And they have to give the quarterback reliable options when the defense takes away the deeper routes. It’s a position that demands intelligence, timing, and toughness. Raymond has the experience, and that could make him the more straightforward choice if Chicago needs somebody to step in and handle extra snaps immediately.

But there’s another name worth watching, and this one gets the excitement level up a notch. Zavion Thomas.

Thomas brings a completely different element to this offense. The report notes that he ran a 4.28-second 40-yard dash at the 2026 NFL Combine. Four point two eight. That’s serious, serious speed, Bears fans. That’s the kind of speed that makes safeties nervous before the ball is even snapped. And when you’ve got a receiver who can threaten defenses vertically, it doesn’t just create opportunities for him. It creates space for everybody else on the field. Safeties back up. Coverage loosens. Underneath routes open up.

But here’s the catch, and we need to be honest about it. Thomas hasn’t had a major role in the passing game so far. According to the report, he’s run routes on just 13 percent of Chicago’s passing plays this season. Thirteen percent. So we need to keep expectations realistic. Speed alone doesn’t make somebody a dependable NFL receiver. Timing matters. Route running matters. Understanding defensive coverages matters. The NFL is full of guys who were lightning fast at the Combine and never figured out the rest of it.

Still, if the coaching staff believes Thomas is ready, this could become a real opportunity for him to show more of what he can do. Sometimes an injury opens a door, and a young player sprints right through it. Literally, in this case.

And that’s where I think Ben Johnson’s approach becomes so important. If Burden misses time, Chicago shouldn’t ask one replacement to become an exact copy of him. That’s a mistake a lot of teams make. Nobody is Luther Burden except Luther Burden. Instead, the smart play is to spread it around. Use Raymond’s experience underneath. Use Thomas’s speed when it makes sense to stretch the field. Keep Rome Odunze heavily involved as a primary receiving option. Continue finding ways to get Colston Loveland the ball at tight end. And keep D’Andre Swift involved out of the backfield as a pass catcher.

That’s how you manage an injury without letting the entire offense lose its rhythm. And if there’s one offensive mind in the NFL who knows how to scheme around personnel and get the ball to the right players in the right spots, it’s Ben Johnson.

Now, to be clear, the report projects Raymond moving into a larger receiving role and Thomas potentially getting more snaps. But that’s a projection, not an official depth chart. For now, the biggest unknown is Burden’s recovery. And as much as I want to see him back out there making plays, the right answer is to let the medical process decide when he’s ready.

Now let’s flip over to the defensive side of the ball, because the Bears have reportedly evaluated a veteran defensive tackle, and this story involves a lot more than whether he can help stop the run.

Chicago brought Jordan Elliott to Halas Hall for a workout on Tuesday. According to NBC Chicago, Elliott is a former third-round pick who has played parts of seven NFL seasons. Seven seasons, with three different organizations: the Cleveland Browns, the San Francisco 49ers, and the Tennessee Titans. He’s appeared in 102 career games, and the report credits him with 159 tackles and five sacks. This season, he recorded 11 tackles in five games with Tennessee before the Titans released him in October.

So let’s be clear about what we’re talking about here. We’re talking about an experienced defensive lineman. Not an unknown prospect. Not some camp body. A guy who has been in NFL locker rooms for seven years, who has lined up against some of the best offensive linemen in the league, and who knows what it takes to survive in the trenches.

But we’re also not talking about a player who would automatically transform Chicago’s defense. His potential value would be as an experienced contributor who could provide depth along the interior. And right now, that depth matters more than ever.

Think about where this Bears defensive line stands. Jeffery Simmons is here, and he’s already been a wrecking ball. But behind him, the injuries are piling up. Kentavius Street has what looks like a serious knee injury. Grady Jarrett has been banged up with a groin problem. We talked about it before the trade deadline: the Bears could really use a DT4 type, a reliable veteran body who can soak up snaps and keep the rotation fresh. And now here’s Chicago bringing in exactly that kind of player for a workout. That’s not a coincidence. That’s a front office reading the injury report and planning ahead.

Now, about that title. Could Jordan Elliott be the next Akiem Hicks? Let’s have some fun with that for a second, because it’s worth thinking about. When Akiem Hicks came to Chicago back in 2016, he wasn’t a household name either. He was a big veteran defensive lineman who’d bounced around, a guy a lot of people saw as a solid rotational piece. And then in Chicago, he became a force. A Pro Bowler. The heart and soul of that defensive front. A player who made opposing offensive lines miserable and gave this city some of its best defensive football in years.

Now, I want to be realistic. Elliott’s numbers so far don’t scream “future Pro Bowler.” Five career sacks across seven seasons tells you he’s not a pass-rushing star. But here’s the thing about the Hicks story: sometimes a veteran just needs the right fit. The right scheme. The right coaching. The right teammates around him. And lining up next to Jeffery Simmons, with Montez Sweat on the edge, with a defensive coordinator like Dennis Allen calling the shots, a big body who can hold his ground inside could find himself in a much better situation than he’s ever been in. Nobody’s saying he’ll become Akiem Hicks overnight. But the idea of a veteran finding new life in Chicago? That’s happened before, and Bears fans remember it well.

And that brings us to the reason this workout is getting so much attention. Because according to the reporting, Elliott’s departure from his last stop came with some controversy attached. And whenever a player comes in with that kind of situation in his recent past, it’s fair to ask the tough questions.

Here’s how I think about it. Every front office in the NFL has to weigh two things when it brings in a veteran like this. Number one: can he help us on the field? And number two: does he fit what we’re building in the locker room? Because those two questions don’t always have the same answer.

On the field, the case is simple. He’s experienced. He’s healthy enough to work out. He’s played 102 games. And the Bears have a real need for interior depth with Street and Jarrett dealing with injuries. A veteran like Elliott could slide in as a rotational piece and give the starters a breather on early downs. That’s valuable in November and December, when the games get physical and the bodies start breaking down.

But the locker room question is where it gets more complicated. This Bears team is building something. You can see it in how Ben Johnson talks at the podium. You can see it in how he handled a costly shoving-match penalty after the Tampa game, with one dry sentence that made the standard crystal clear. Discipline matters in this building. Culture matters. And every new player who walks through the door either strengthens that culture or tests it.

So should Chicago take the chance? I’ll give you my honest take. A workout is not a contract. Bringing a player in to look at him, to talk to him, to evaluate him up close, is exactly what a responsible front office should do. Ryan Poles and his staff get to see the player in person. They get to ask the questions directly. They get to hear his side of the story. They get to measure whether he’s someone who would fit in with this group or someone who would be a distraction. That’s homework, and homework is good.

If the Bears decide he’s the right fit, both as a player and as a person, then you bring him in as low-risk depth, probably on a modest deal, and see what he’s got. And if they decide he’s not, then they move on, and nobody’s worse off. The key is that the evaluation goes both ways. Talent alone isn’t enough. Not on a team with real playoff aspirations. Not in a locker room that’s been building chemistry all season long.

And remember, this is a front office that has been aggressive this year. They traded two first-round picks for Jeffery Simmons. They explored deals for cornerbacks and pass rushers at the deadline. They’ve been checking every corner of the market for players who can make this team better. A workout for a veteran defensive tackle fits perfectly with that mindset. Poles is leaving no stone unturned. And with the bye week here, this is exactly the right time to do it.

So here’s where I land on both stories. On offense, the Luther Burden concussion is the most important thing to watch over the next two weeks. The bye gives him time, but the medical process has to lead the way, and if he needs more time, Kalif Raymond and Zavion Thomas are the names to watch, with Ben Johnson spreading the ball around to Odunze, Loveland, and Swift. On defense, Jordan Elliott’s workout shows a front office that’s taking the injuries on the interior seriously and looking for veteran depth to support Jeffery Simmons. Whether he becomes a long-term piece, or even the next Akiem Hicks, remains to be seen. But the Bears are doing their due diligence, and that’s exactly what you want.

First, let’s talk about what to watch in that Tampa Bay game, because it might be the most important regular season audition of the year. Number one, injuries. Every single snap, every player who walks off the field slowly, every player who heads into the medical tent, that’s going to shape what the front office does next. And number two, the overall play, especially at a spot like outside corner. Do Tyrique Stevenson or Jaylon Johnson show any signs of life? Do they give you real confidence that they can hold up for the rest of the season? Because the answer to that question is going to shape just how aggressive the Bears need to be before Tuesday. If those corners show up, maybe Poles makes a smaller move. If they get picked apart, the phone calls start and they don’t stop.

Now let me outline the needs, because before you can talk about targets, you have to know what you’re shopping for.

The top two needs are clear. Number one: outside cornerback. Number two: edge help. And on the edge, it’s not necessarily about finding a superstar, especially after adding Simmons. With Simmons wreaking havoc on the interior, you don’t need another blue-chip pass rusher to change this defense. But you could absolutely use another piece. Another body, another threat, another guy who forces offenses to pick their poison.

Number three on the list: a tight end. Specifically, a number three tight end who’s a blocking type. That’s not the sexy need. That’s not the move that’s going to lead SportsCenter. But it’s the kind of depth that helps a team in November and December when the weather turns and the running game matters more than ever.

And number four: defensive tackle, with an asterisk. Here’s why. Kentavius Street has what looks like a serious knee injury. And Grady Jarrett is banged up with a groin problem. Obviously, you’ve got Simmons now, and that’s a massive upgrade on the interior. But depth matters. You could use a DT4 type, a reliable veteran body who can soak up snaps and keep the rotation fresh. So keep a very close eye on those injury reports, because if the news gets worse, that asterisk disappears and defensive tackle jumps right up the list.

Now, here’s a helpful way to think about all of this: price tiers. Because every name on this list comes with a cost, and the cost matters just as much as the player.

At the bottom tier, you’ve got the players who would cost a seventh-round pick or a simple pick swap. Nahshon Wright is the perfect example of that tier. In the middle tier, you’ve got players who would cost a fifth or sixth-round pick. That’s where you’ll find names like Carl Granderson, Rasul Douglas, and Riq Woolen. And at the top tier, you’ve got the players who would cost a second-round pick or more. That’s where Tuli Tuipulotu and Josh Sweat live.

And here’s the key point. The Bears used two first-round picks to get Jeffery Simmons. Two first-rounders. That’s a massive investment. So the realistic shopping zone for Chicago is the bottom and the middle. That doesn’t mean the top tier is completely impossible, but it does mean any move up there would have to be extraordinary. A smart general manager spends the next dollar where it buys the most improvement. And for a team that has already made its big bet, that means filling holes cheaply and efficiently. That’s the game Ryan Poles is playing right now.

Alright, let’s begin with the cornerbacks, because this is the position that’s got everybody in Chicago on edge.

First up, Riq Woolen. Now, Woolen is a name we’ve talked about before, and he’s a very boom-or-bust player. He’s made some good plays this season. He’s also been beaten a lot. And honestly, that’s kind of who he’s been ever since his rookie season in Seattle. And what a rookie season that was. As a sixth-round pick, he was awesome. He came out of nowhere and played like a star. But it’s been up and down ever since.

He’s also not the most willing tackler. And come on, who does that remind you of? Bears fans know exactly what I’m talking about. But here’s the thing: Woolen does have more speed than Jaylon Johnson, and I think he’d be a decent scheme fit in Chicago. He’s on a one-year deal, which makes him a low-risk rental. Could you get him for a sixth-round pick? If so, I’d be open-minded. Let’s be honest about it, though. Is Riq Woolen going to save your cornerback room? No. Is he an upgrade? Probably. Even if it’s not a huge one, an upgrade is an upgrade when you’re trying to win now.

Next, there’s a veteran corner on the Chargers, a guy in his early thirties who’s still playing good football. He’s historically been more of a zone corner, but over the last two seasons combined, he’s snagged nine interceptions. Nine. So this guy makes plays on the football, which is exactly what the Bears have been missing. Now, how would defensive coordinator Dennis Allen view him as a fit? That’s a fair question, and I’m honestly not sure. I also don’t know whether the Chargers are going to trade any of these guys at all, and we’ll get to why in a minute. So keep that in mind with every Chargers name you hear.

Then there’s the reunion story. Nahshon Wright. Ian Rapoport mentioned on Tuesday that the Bears could reach out to the Jets and bring him back. And look, Wright was the definition of boom or bust with the Bears last year. According to the numbers, he gave up a lot of yardage and six touchdowns. That’s the bust side. But here’s the boom side, and it’s impressive. Eleven pass breakups. Five interceptions. Two forced fumbles. And a fumble recovery. That’s a playmaker. That’s a guy who changes games, for better or worse.

He played his best football under Al Harris, the Bears’ cornerbacks coach, and I’m sure he’d welcome the chance to come back. Think about it from his perspective. He’s barely playing in New York. Really, he’s not playing at all. Zero defensive snaps. Inactive in five games. If you’re Nahshon Wright, a phone call from Chicago sounds like a lifeline. And the price? Probably a seventh-round pick or a simple pick swap. That’s about as cheap as it gets for a player with that kind of ball production.

Now, a brand new name has popped up: Mike Sainristil. Albert Breer reported that Sainristil could be available from the Washington Commanders. He had a pretty good rookie year, but back then he was playing more in the slot than on the outside. Since then, Washington has played him primarily on the outside, and honestly, from a Bears standpoint, he’s been pretty rough there. And that’s the problem. If his best position is the slot, and he’s struggled on the outside, that doesn’t really help Chicago’s cause. The need is outside corner. So Sainristil is a name out there, and it’s worth knowing, but I don’t think he solves your problems.

And finally, Rasul Douglas. We talked about him a bit at last year’s deadline too, and maybe this is the year you revisit it. He’s a veteran on a cheap one-year deal, and he might cost a little more than a player like Wright, maybe a sixth-round pick. Here’s how I’d describe him: a steady CB2. Is he great? No. Is he awful? No. He can hold his own. He’d be pretty cheap, and he could help you get through the rest of the season. Sometimes that’s exactly what a contender needs. Not a superstar, just a reliable guy who doesn’t get you beat. He may be worth a look.

Before we move on to the edge, I want to make an important point about the cornerback position, because it changes how you should think about this whole deadline.

The Bears have two starters, Stevenson and Johnson. And the question isn’t whether they’re bad. The question is whether they’ve been consistent enough to trust. That’s a big difference. And here’s the thing about defense in the NFL: a good defense with a strong front can hide a shaky secondary for a while. Why? Because the pass rush shortens the time a quarterback has to find an open receiver. If the quarterback has to get rid of the ball in two seconds, your corners don’t have to cover for five.

And with Simmons lining up next to the pass rushers, that cover is stronger than it’s ever been. So yes, the need at corner is real. But it’s also the kind of need that a cheap, versatile veteran can address, because the front is already doing part of the work. You don’t necessarily need a shutdown superstar. You need a guy who’s dependable. That’s why names like Wright and Douglas make so much sense at their price points.

Now, let’s move to the edge, and let’s start with the biggest name of them all. Khalil Mack.

Reports continue to suggest that Chicago is interested. But will it actually happen? That’s the million-dollar question. For whatever reason, the Los Angeles Chargers do not want to sell, despite being 0 and 7. Zero wins. Seven losses. And they still don’t want to sell.

Now, let’s be real about Mack. We’ve said it all along: he’s not the same player he was when Chicago traded for him back in 2018. He’s 35 years old. But he’s still a productive player. He’s a plus run defender, and he can still get after the passer, especially when you leave him in one-on-one situations. And guess what? With Simmons on the roster, he’d get plenty of those one-on-ones. Offenses would have to slide protection toward Simmons on the inside, and that leaves the edges exposed. We already saw what Montez Sweat could do when less attention was focused on him. Imagine Mack getting those same opportunities.

But the ball is in the Chargers’ court. The insiders say they don’t want to sell because they believe they can turn the season around. At 0 and 7. And not even close in most of those games. I’m still interested, and I think the Bears are still interested. But whether it actually happens remains to be seen. I’ll be honest with you: I was more confident 24 to 48 hours ago. Right now, it’s anybody’s guess.

Now, there’s a logic to the Chargers’ reluctance that’s worth understanding. A team that’s 0 and 7 usually sells. That’s just how the NFL works. But sellers also look at the return. If the best offer for a 35-year-old pass rusher is a late-round pick, the team may decide it’s just not worth the trouble, especially if they still believe they can turn things around. I think that belief is misplaced, but it explains why the insiders keep saying Los Angeles doesn’t want to sell. And the practical effect for Chicago is this: the price might not drop as the deadline approaches. The Bears may need to decide whether to pay a little more, or move on to the next name.

Speaking of the Chargers, Mack’s teammate Tuli Tuipulotu has also been mentioned. But he’s going to cost at least a day two pick. And considering the Bears just gave up two first-round picks for Simmons, I don’t expect Chicago to be in that market. On top of the trade cost, you’d almost certainly have to extend him. If I’m the Chargers, I’m holding out for a high price, probably a second-round pick. And that makes sense from their side. Tuipulotu is 25 years old and in the final year of his contract. He’s young, he’s productive, and he’s going to want to get paid. But if I’m the Bears, I’m not doing that. Not given the capital I just gave up for Simmons. I think the Simmons trade was worth it. But you don’t give up a second-rounder on top of it.

I also want to point out that edge help is not a luxury. Even with a star interior lineman, a pass rush benefits from another threat on the outside. Why? Because it forces offenses to choose where to put their attention. If you’ve only got one or two guys who can win, offenses double them and dare everybody else to beat them. But if you’ve got threats everywhere, somebody’s going to be left one-on-one, and somebody’s going to win. A veteran who can set the edge against the run and win some one-on-one matchups would give the Bears so much more flexibility. That’s why I keep coming back to Mack and Carl Granderson as the most natural fits. Both can play the run. Both can rush the passer in spurts. And both would likely cost a lot less than the younger stars.

Let’s talk about one of those younger stars: Josh Sweat. Now, I was interested in Sweat this summer. I’ll admit it. But I’ve cooled on him a decent amount, especially after the Simmons move. He has started to pick things up in Arizona, with three sacks in his last five games after a slow start. But here’s the problem. Reports suggest the Cardinals want a high day two pick, which tells me they’re asking for a second-rounder. And that’s a complete non-starter. I wouldn’t even trade a third-round pick for him. If you told me you could get him for a fourth or a fifth, okay, now we can have a conversation. But a second or a third? Absolutely not.

Now, Carl Granderson. And I think this might be the most interesting name on the entire list. Early in the year, the Saints felt like a fun NFC South contender, and in theory, they’re not out of it. They’re only a game and a half back. So would New Orleans part with a guy like Granderson? He’s about 30 years old, a rugged edge defender, and here’s the big one: he’s got familiarity with Dennis Allen. That connection matters. He’s probably the one guy on this list who’d be most open-minded about coming to Chicago. He fits the scheme. He’s a good run defender who can make a splash as a pass rusher from time to time. And he may not cost a ton. If you could get him for a fifth or a sixth, that would be great. And honestly, that’s about where I am on Mack as well.

There’s also Jermaine Johnson on the board. But the Bears already made a trade with the Jets when it comes to Simmons, and it seems like New York wants to keep Johnson. He’s a free agent after this season and a young player who’ll be looking for an extension. So I don’t think that one’s realistic.

So now the big question: what’s the bigger need, cornerback or edge? Type CB or EDGE in the comments right now.

Here’s my take. If all things are equal, cornerback is the bigger need. But all things aren’t equal, because there seem to be more edge options available out there. So it really comes down to value and availability. Who can you get, and what does it cost? The smartest front offices don’t just chase the biggest need. They chase the best deal that improves the team the most.

And that brings us back to the idea of waiting. The Bears play one more game before the deadline, and the coaching staff can use that game to evaluate. If the corners play well against Tampa Bay, the urgency drops. If they get picked apart, the phone calls start. And the same goes for the injured defensive tackles. If the injury report brings more bad news about Kentavius Street or Grady Jarrett, a late-round veteran defensive tackle suddenly becomes a lot more attractive.

The deadline is a moving target. And the best front offices keep their options open until the last practical moment. Ryan Poles has already shown us he’s not afraid to make the big move. Now we’re going to find out if he’s just as good at making the smart, small moves that turn a good team into a dangerous one.

At corner, Nahshon Wright is the cheap reunion option, Rasul Douglas is the steady veteran, Riq Woolen is the boom-or-bust gamble, and Mike Sainristil probably isn’t the answer. On the edge, Khalil Mack is still the dream, but the 0-and-7 Chargers don’t want to sell. Carl Granderson might be the sneaky perfect fit. And Josh Sweat and Tuli Tuipulotu are simply too expensive after the Simmons deal.

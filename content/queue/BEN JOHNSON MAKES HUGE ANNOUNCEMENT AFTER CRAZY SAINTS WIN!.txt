Now, one of the most interesting parts of this press conference was when Johnson explained why this pass rush has suddenly become so dangerous. And he broke it down in a really clear way. He said every game tells its own story in terms of how much an opponent wants to run the ball or throw it. But over the last few weeks, the Bears have been able to build leads. And when you build leads, you turn the other team a little more one-dimensional. They have to throw. And when they have to throw, the pass rush gets to pin its ears back and go.

But it's not just about the scoreboard. Johnson gave a ton of credit to DA, defensive coordinator Dennis Allen. He said Allen does a great job putting pressure on protection schemes, especially with the mugged-up looks he likes to major in. For those who don't know, a mugged-up look is when linebackers or defensive backs crowd the line of scrimmage, showing blitz, so the offense can't tell who's rushing and who's dropping into coverage. It's a chess move. It confuses the offensive line, it confuses the quarterback, and it forces the protection to make guesses.

And according to Johnson, those looks end up giving the Bears' pass rushers a lot of one-on-one opportunities. And they're taking advantage of them. Every single week.

Then he summed up this defense's identity in one sentence. If they can get offenses off schedule, second and long, third downs, that's where this defense is at its best. Think about that. This is a defense that wants you in obvious passing situations. It wants you to have to drop back. Because when you do, the sacks come in bunches.

A reporter asked him about those six sacks and how much of it was situational, like rushing the passer in the fourth quarter with a lead, versus pure performance. Johnson said he'd have to look at how they were distributed throughout the game, but it felt like the Bears were able to affect the passer for most of the day. Not just in garbage time. Not just in one quarter. For most of the day. That's dominance.

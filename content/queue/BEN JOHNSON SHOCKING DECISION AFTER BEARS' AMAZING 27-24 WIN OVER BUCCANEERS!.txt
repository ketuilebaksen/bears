BEN JOHNSON SHOCKING DECISION AFTER BEARS’ AMAZING 27-24 WIN OVER BUCCANEERS!

Two for twelve. That’s the number. Two conversions on twelve third-down attempts. And that’s the first thing Ben Johnson put on the table in the opening minute of his postgame press conference. Not the final score. Not the pick six. Not the game-winning kick. Two for twelve. And then he added a line that tells you exactly how he feels about it: that it seems to be showing up each and every week right now.

He said it without any drama. No raised voice. No frustration boiling over. Almost like a man reading a weather report he’s seen way too many times. And yet, this was the same coach standing at the podium after a win, talking about a team that found a way. A 27 to 24 victory over a good Tampa Bay Buccaneers team. A bad number sitting right next to a good result. And folks, that contrast is the entire story of this press conference.

Let’s start where Ben Johnson started: the health report. And it was brief. Two injuries of note. Tyrique Stevenson with a thumb injury, and Luther Burden with a concussion. “We’ll see where they’re at.” That was the extent of it.

Now, that’s standard coach language, and I wouldn’t read too much into it just yet. But let’s think about these two injuries for a second. Concussions, in particular, are always a wait-and-see situation. There’s a protocol. There are steps. There’s no rushing it, and there shouldn’t be. And a thumb injury is one of those strange ones that can either be a nuisance for weeks or turn out to be no problem at all. For a cornerback who needs his hands to jam receivers and fight for the ball at the catch point, it’s definitely one to monitor.

What stood out to me was how matter-of-fact Ben Johnson was about it. Nothing dramatic. Nothing hidden. Just the facts as he knew them in that moment. And here’s the good news: the timing really helps. The Bears are heading into the bye week. That gives both injured players an extra week to heal, and it gives the coaching staff time to evaluate what this team looks like at full strength. If both Stevenson and Burden return after the bye, this group is in much better shape than the box score from this game suggests.

Then Ben Johnson turned to the football. And the first thing he did was give respect to the opponent. He called Tampa Bay a good football team. He said their defense was outstanding, and that it showed throughout the game. I really like that he started there, because it frames everything that followed. When a coach tells you the other side was good, he’s not making excuses. He’s giving you the context for why the game felt so hard. This wasn’t a bad Bears team stumbling against a bad opponent. This was a Bears team going toe to toe with a quality defense and finding a way.

And this is where you start to see how Ben Johnson thinks. Because coaches who win ugly have two easy options. Option one: pretend the ugliness didn’t happen. Smile, celebrate, talk about the win, and move on. Option two: overreact. Walk into the locker room and tell everybody that everything is broken. Ben Johnson did neither. He said the win was good. He said the team they played was good. He said his defense was outstanding. And he said the offense had problems that need to be fixed. All at the same time.

Holding all of those ideas together is a lot harder than it sounds. And it’s a mark of how he runs the room. Balanced. Honest. Steady.

Now let’s get into the offense, because Ben Johnson’s description was refreshingly honest. He said the unit started off hot, then really cooled down through the middle of the game, and then found a way at the very end, when it was needed most, to get into field goal range.

Think about that as a three-act story. Act one: a fast start. Act two: a long, painful stall. Act three: a clutch finish. And that two-for-twelve third-down number explains act two perfectly. A team that converts two of twelve third downs is going to have a really hard time keeping the football. That puts pressure on the defense, and it takes all the rhythm away from the offense. Ben Johnson knows it, and he said it plainly.

And I want to connect that third-down number to the bigger picture, because I think it’s the quiet villain of this entire game. When you go two for twelve, your offense is on the field for a few snaps and then it’s right back off again. Three and out. Punt. Three and out. Punt. And your defense never gets to rest. Now combine that with five turnovers, five times handing the other team the ball, often with a short field. You can start to see why Ben Johnson spent so much time praising how his defense handled sudden-change situations. The offense was handing the defense one brutal assignment after another.

But the number that really drove the night was five. Five turnovers. Ben Johnson said it twice. Once in his opening remarks, and again when he described the message he gave to the locker room. When you have five turnovers, it’s hard to win, he said. And yet this team still found a way.

He didn’t pretend the night was clean. He told his players directly that they didn’t play as well as they’re capable of playing. But then he followed that with a line I think is really worth repeating. He said he believes the best is yet to come for this team’s football in the back half of the season.

Let that sink in. That’s a coach acknowledging a flawed performance while flat-out refusing to let it become a verdict on his team. That’s not denial. That’s belief. And when your head coach believes the best is still ahead after a five-turnover win, that tells you how much confidence he has in what this team can become.

Now here’s the decision that a lot of people might have missed, and I think it says a lot about Ben Johnson as a leader. He laid out the plan for the bye week. After the film session and a couple of days of cleaning things up, he wants to get the players away from the coaches for a bit. In his words, they need some rest, and they need to get away from these coaches.

It’s a small moment of humor. But it’s also a window into how he manages people. A lot of coaches, after a five-turnover performance, would grind their players into the ground. Extra meetings. Extra film. Extra punishment. Ben Johnson is going the other way. He knows the difference between work and recovery, and he’s willing to give his players the second one once the first one has been done. That’s not soft. That’s smart. Fresh legs and fresh minds in November are worth more than one more film session on a game that’s already over.

And it fits perfectly with how he talked about the offense as a whole. He didn’t blame the quarterback. He didn’t blame the offensive line. He said the team handled pressure well early, and not later. He said the turnovers were the problem. And he said he’d talk with Caleb tomorrow. The result is a very calm public posture. And I think that calm is completely deliberate. A quarterback who’s had a rough night doesn’t need his coach adding noise. He needs a plan for the next day.

Which brings us to the most sensitive part of the press conference: Caleb Williams.

A reporter brought up the fact that Caleb calls himself an “eraser” when plays break down, a guy who can make something out of nothing, and noted that he threw a couple of his interceptions in exactly those situations. So what was Ben Johnson’s message to him?

The answer was almost disarmingly simple. He said he hadn’t had a chance to sit down and talk with him yet. That’s what tomorrow was for. And later, when he was asked what he tells Caleb in the moment to steer him back on track, he said it’s just to get back on the horse and keep going.

I find that so refreshing. No public scolding. No big breakdown at the podium. No throwing his quarterback under the bus in front of the cameras. Just a plan to have a private conversation after everybody’s had a chance to breathe. In a league where quarterbacks get dissected in real time, every throw, every decision, every facial expression, that’s an incredibly mature way to handle it. Caleb Williams is the franchise. And Ben Johnson is protecting the franchise while still making sure the mistakes get fixed behind closed doors.

Now, here’s a moment I think went right over a lot of people’s heads. A reporter brought up the third possession of the game, where there was a shoving match that resulted in a 15-yard penalty. The question was whether Ben Johnson said anything to the player as he came off to the sideline.

His answer? He said he didn’t know that the player played on offense after that.

Read that again. That’s about as dry as an answer can possibly get. And it tells you exactly what he thought of that play without making a single speech about it. No lecture. No finger-pointing at the podium. Just a quiet, ice-cold message. A disciplined team doesn’t give the opponent free yards, and a flag in a shoving match is the exact opposite of discipline. Ben Johnson didn’t need to make a speech. With one sentence, he made the standard crystal clear. And everybody in that locker room heard it.

That’s the kind of thing that builds a culture. Not yelling. Consequences. Standards. And a coach who means what he says.

Let’s talk about the second half, because Ben Johnson was just as direct there. A reporter asked what Tampa Bay’s defense did to throw off the offense. And his answer was simple. They knew the Bucs were going to bring a lot of pressure. They knew the offense would need to handle it. And the offense did a good job of that early in the game, and not later on. It was really as simple as that, he said.

I appreciate the lack of mystery there. There was no secret scheme. No new wrinkle the Bears had never seen. It was a good defense bringing pressure, and the Bears’ protection and recognition stopped holding up. That’s an honest answer, and it’s also an encouraging one, because it’s fixable. Protection and recognition can be coached. They can be cleaned up on tape. And with a full bye week, the staff has time to do exactly that.

Now, where Ben Johnson really got animated was when he talked about the defense in sudden-change situations. That, he said, was really the story of the game. The defense stepping up again and again and again, all the way until the last one, when Tampa Bay finally broke through and scored a touchdown. But on the four before that, he said the defense did an outstanding job rising to the occasion.

And then he said the line I love most from this entire press conference. They don’t bat an eye. They don’t blink.

He gave credit to DA, defensive coordinator Dennis Allen, for preaching that mentality. But he also said the players take a lot of pride in it. And then he added three simple words: we needed it.

That’s the clearest summary of the whole night. The offense kept giving the ball away, and the defense kept answering the bell.

And I want to stay on that phrase for a second, because “they don’t blink” says so much. Think about what a sudden-change moment actually looks like. The ball gets turned over. The offense is stunned, walking off the field with their heads down. The crowd is groaning. And the defense is expected to sprint out there, with zero time to prepare, often with the opponent sitting in great field position, and hold. It’s one of the hardest assignments in all of football, because there’s no time to settle in. There’s no time to adjust. You just have to go.

Four straight stops under those conditions is not a fluke. It’s a habit. And habits come from coaching and pride. Ben Johnson mentioned both. That’s a defense with an identity. And in November, December, and January, identity wins football games.

Then there was the warmest answer of the entire night. A reporter asked about Devin Bush. And Ben Johnson lit up. He said Bush keeps getting better every single week. And then, speaking off the cuff, while noting that he still needed to look at the tape, he said this might have been Bush’s best game yet as a Bear.

He described him as being all over the place. Physical when he hits guys. Fast. A great coverage player. And then came the line I think matters most. He said Bush’s understanding of the defense is what allows him to play instinctive, fast football.

That’s a very specific compliment, and it’s worth understanding. Speed without understanding gets a player beat. He’ll fly to the wrong spot, bite on the wrong fake, take himself out of the play. And understanding without speed gets a player left behind. He’ll know where to go but never get there in time. When a coach says a player has both, and that the understanding is still growing, you’re hearing about a defender who’s still on the way up. That’s how coaches talk about a player who has stopped thinking and started playing. And for Bears fans, that’s incredibly exciting.

Now let’s get to the play of the game. The pick six. The Bears were leading 17 to 13, and that lead was very much in jeopardy when Jaylon Johnson made his big play. A reporter pointed out that the defense had been dealing with a lot of injuries at cornerback and asked how big of a boost that play was.

Ben Johnson said it was a big play all around. You could feel the momentum swing back in the Bears’ favor. And then look at how he handed out the credit. He gave credit to DA for a great call, mixing up the coverages. He said Jaylon was in a great spot. And once again, he pointed to Montez Sweat for leading the way with the lead block that sprang Jaylon free.

The coordinator’s call. The player’s positioning. The teammate’s block. That’s a play that belongs to everyone. And that’s exactly how championship teams talk about their big moments. Nobody’s bigger than the team.

And I wouldn’t underestimate how much that moment mattered for the rest of the game. A pick six changes the scoreboard, sure. But it also changes the mood. Ben Johnson said you could feel the momentum swing, and I think that’s the right way to measure it. Five turnovers can wear on a sideline. It can drain the energy out of a team. But one big defensive touchdown can reset all of it in a single play. And that’s exactly what happened.

Special teams got a mention too. The game ended on the right foot of Cairo Santos, and Ben Johnson said he liked what he saw from the return game. He said that was an area the team felt needed improvement early in the season, and the returners showed up and made some plays. That’s a coach noticing progress in a unit people usually ignore until the final kick. And in close games, those hidden yards in the return game can be the difference between a win and a loss.

And then, the final drive. A reporter noted that the Bears hadn’t needed Caleb Williams to orchestrate any late fourth-quarter drives until this game, and asked what Ben Johnson saw on that last drive, the drive that got Cairo Santos into position to win it.

Ben Johnson agreed with the premise. It hasn’t happened a whole lot this year. And then he said something really telling. Despite the displeasure he might be showing at the podium, there’s a lot of good in a win like this, because it’s hard to win under those circumstances. He said he felt prepared and ready. And as a play caller, he felt just as calm on that last drive as he did all game long.

And then: we thrive in those situations.

That’s not a throwaway line. Play callers are under real, intense pressure in the last two minutes of a close game. Every call matters. Every second matters. And the play callers who feel steady in those moments tend to call steady drives. If that calmness carries into the next close game, and the one after that, it’s an asset this team didn’t even know it had before this night. A clutch final drive with your quarterback coming off a four-interception game? That’s resilience. That’s growth. That’s a team learning how to win.

So when I put all of these answers together, the picture is clear. This is a coach who cares more about process than outcome. He talked about learning from the tape. About the private conversation with his quarterback. About the defense’s mentality. About giving his players rest. He never really celebrated the win. He treated it like a data point. And to me, that’s exactly why the Bears look steadier than their offensive numbers suggest.

It wasn’t a pretty win, and Ben Johnson never tried to dress it up. Five turnovers. Two of twelve on third down. A quiet, frustrating stretch in the middle of the game. But the defense didn’t blink. The special teams contributed. And a quarterback who had a rough night with the football still got his team into field goal range when the moment came. A coach looks at all of that and sees a team that can handle a close fourth quarter. And that’s a quality you simply cannot manufacture in practice.

One last thing I want to flag: how often Ben Johnson mentioned the tape. He said they’d learn from it. He said he’d look at it before calling it Bush’s best game. He said the team would clean things up over the next couple of days. A coach who keeps pointing to the film is a coach who wants evidence, not impressions. And that’s exactly the right habit for a team trying to fix turnovers and third-down struggles during a bye week.

Let’s wrap it up. The Bears beat Tampa Bay 27 to 24 despite five turnovers and a two-for-twelve night on third down. Ben Johnson was honest about the problems, calm about his quarterback, ice-cold about a costly penalty, and full of praise for a defense that refused to blink, for Devin Bush’s growth, for Jaylon Johnson’s game-changing pick six, and for a final drive that showed this team can win when it matters most. Tyrique Stevenson and Luther Burden are banged up, but the bye week couldn’t come at a better time. And Ben Johnson’s decision to clean things up and then send his players away to rest might be exactly what this team needs for the stretch run.

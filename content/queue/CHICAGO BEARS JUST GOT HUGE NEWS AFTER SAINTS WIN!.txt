Now, the second piece of huge news is about why this defense is suddenly so dominant. And Ben Johnson gave us the blueprint.

He explained that every game tells its own story, depending on how much an opponent wants to run and how much it wants to throw. But over the last few weeks, a pattern has formed. The Bears have been building leads. And when you build leads, the other team becomes more one-dimensional. They can't run the ball as much because they're playing from behind. They have to throw. And once they have to throw, Chicago's pass rush gets to go hunting.

But that's only half of it. The other half is Dennis Allen. Johnson gave his defensive coordinator a ton of credit, saying DA does a great job putting pressure on protection schemes with the mugged-up looks he loves to use.

Let me explain what that means, because it's the secret sauce of this defense. A mugged-up look is when defenders crowd the line of scrimmage before the snap. Linebackers creep up into the gaps. Sometimes defensive backs walk down too. The offense looks at it and sees a possible blitz from everywhere. Now the offensive line has to guess. Who's coming? Who's dropping back? Who do we block?

And here's the magic: no matter how they guess, somebody usually ends up blocked one-on-one. Johnson said exactly that. Those looks give the Bears' pass rushers a lot of one-on-one opportunities, and the rushers are taking full advantage.

Then he gave us the line that defines this whole defense. If the Bears can get an offense off schedule, second and long, third downs, that's where they're at their best.

Think about the math there. On first down, the offense has options. Run it, throw it, play action, whatever they want. But on second and long, or third and seven, or third and ten, the offense has to throw. Everybody in the stadium knows it. And that's when Dennis Allen dials up the pressure, the mugged-up looks come out, and the quarterback ends up on his back. Six times on Sunday.

A reporter asked whether those six sacks were mostly situational, piling up late in the game with the Bears ahead, or whether it was pure performance. Johnson said he'd need to look at how they were spread out, but it felt like the Bears were affecting the quarterback for most of the day. That's the key. This wasn't garbage-time padding. This was pressure from start to finish.

A reporter asked Johnson about Simmons's explosive plays and those big celebrations, and how that energy affects the whole team, including the coaches on the sideline. And Johnson's answer was something every Bears fan needs to hear. He said Simmons is quickly becoming a voice that everyone hears and everyone rallies behind.

Let that sink in. Simmons hasn't been in Chicago very long. He arrived in a midseason trade. He's still learning names, still learning the building, still getting to know his teammates. And already, the whole locker room is following him.

And then Johnson shared what happened after the game. Simmons received a game ball. And when he spoke to the team, he made a statement. He said he was tired of hearing the coaches talk about how good the Saints were as a second-half team. And to be fair, Johnson admitted New Orleans really is a darn good second-half team. But Simmons told the room something simple and powerful: we're a good second-half team, too.

That's not just a quote. That's a mindset. That's a leader refusing to let his team feel like the underdog. That's a player saying, "I don't care how good they are. We're better."

Johnson said the guys really responded to that. And he believes Simmons's influence is only going to grow as he gets to know everybody on a more personal level. He said it's going to keep blossoming.

Now think back to the trade. Ryan Poles gave up two first-round picks for Jeffery Simmons. A lot of people questioned it. Too much, they said. But this is exactly what you pay for. Not just the sacks. Not just the tackles for loss. You pay for the guy who walks into the locker room and changes the temperature. And Simmons is doing that already. When a head coach says the whole team rallies behind your voice after just a few weeks, you've become something special.

Alright, now the fourth piece of huge news. And this one is my favorite story of the week. The rookie redemption.

Let's rewind. One week earlier, against Tampa Bay, rookie wide receiver Zavion Thomas was flagged for unnecessary roughness in the red zone. The Bears had first and goal from the 8, and suddenly it was second and goal from the 21. That drive ended in an interception. And after the game, Ben Johnson delivered one of the most ice-cold quotes of the season. He said he didn't know if Thomas played on offense after that.

A lot of people wondered how a rookie would handle that kind of public message. Would he sulk? Would he lose confidence? Would he fade into the background?

He did none of those things. He went to work.

A reporter asked Johnson how Thomas handled being pulled from the offense. And Johnson's answer was glowing. He said Thomas is a player who continues to learn, and the coaches gain more trust in him every week. He said Thomas has been working hard behind the scenes, with the receivers coaches spending a lot of extra time with him. And over the course of a week of practice, Johnson said, you could see that Thomas was more on it. More focused. More locked in. And as a result, both the coaches and his teammates have more trust in him.

And then came the announcement. Johnson said Thomas's package of plays, the set of plays he goes into each game with, is going to continue to grow.

That is huge news. The head coach is telling the whole league that this rookie's role is expanding. A week after benching him on offense.

Johnson didn't stop there. He said everybody knows how much of a playmaker Thomas can be when the ball is in his hands. Elite speed. And remember, we're talking about a player who ran a 4.28 40-yard dash at the Combine. But then Johnson pointed out something that makes Thomas even more dangerous. For a player that fast, he said, it's really impressive how well Thomas can track the ball, and how natural his hands are. A lot of speedsters can run past defensive backs, but they can't finish the catch. Thomas can do both. And Johnson closed by saying the Bears see a really bright future for him.

And we saw that future on Sunday. Third and long. The Bears had just taken a sack on the previous play. The drive was in trouble. And Caleb Williams went deep to Zavion Thomas for 40 yards. That catch helped put Chicago up by two scores. Johnson called it a big play, precisely because of the situation: third and long, right after a sack, with momentum slipping. That's a clutch catch. From a rookie. Seven days after being benched.

This is why Ben Johnson's coaching style works. He doesn't hide mistakes. He calls them out. But he also gives players the path back. Put in the work, earn the trust, and you'll be rewarded. Thomas did exactly that, and now he's getting more opportunities. That's how you develop young talent. That's how you build a culture.

But now, let's talk about the not-so-good. Because Ben Johnson certainly didn't hide it.

When a reporter asked what he wished had gone differently on offense, Johnson went straight to the running game. He said the Bears weren't as consistent running the ball as they wanted to be coming off the bye. And that stings, because the running game was a point of emphasis during the bye week. They worked on it. They focused on it. And yet, when Johnson looked at the numbers, his runners were under four yards per carry. He also said the team wasn't hitting the efficiency number that the staff tracks closely. And he said they'll dig in to figure out why.

That's a real issue, and it's one to watch closely. As we get deeper into the season, the weather in Chicago gets brutal. Wind, cold, snow. Passing gets harder. The teams that can run the football in December are the teams that win in January. Right now, the Bears' defense is building leads. But to protect those leads and close out games, the offense has to be able to grind on the ground. Getting the run game fixed might be the single biggest key to how far this team goes.

Then there was D'Andre Swift's fumble. A reporter asked if Johnson had anything else to say to Swift about putting the ball on the ground. And his answer was one word: "Nope." He'd already talked to Swift. Done. Handled. Moving on.

And honestly, that's become a pattern with this coach. Mistakes get addressed privately and directly. He doesn't drag players through the media. He doesn't create drama. He fixes it in the building and turns the page.

When asked about Caleb Williams's overall performance, Johnson kept it short too. He said they'd have to look at the tape. Again, no emotional reactions. Just evidence.

On the injury front, there was only one injury of note. Running back Roschon Johnson is dealing with an ankle issue. Ben Johnson said they'd see how it develops as the week goes on. And with the run game already struggling and a very short week ahead, that's one to keep an eye on.

And that respect makes the Bears' defensive performance even more impressive. They weren't facing a quarterback who crumbles under pressure.
